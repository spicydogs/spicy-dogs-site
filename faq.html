About my work

How I approach behaviour.

My work starts with understanding the individual dog rather than applying one protocol to every behaviour that looks the same from the outside.

What kind of dog behaviour consultant are you?

I work from a relationship-based, welfare-focused perspective. I look at behaviour in context: the dog’s emotional state, history, environment, motivations, relationships, learning and individual needs.

I have a particular interest in village and free-living dogs, fearful and anxious dogs, reactive dogs, and dogs whose sensitivity or independence can make conventional pet expectations difficult.

What methods do you use?

There is no single method I apply to every dog. Depending on the case, support may include changes to environment and routine, management, reducing pressure, building predictability and safety, improving communication, changing associations, teaching useful skills and helping guardians respond differently to behaviour.

The plan is built around why the behaviour may be happening and what is realistic for that dog and household.

Do you use punishment or aversive tools?

My work does not centre on punishment, intimidation or forcing a dog through fear. I prioritise welfare, emotional safety, clear communication and strategies that help the dog cope more successfully.

Sometimes behaviour still needs firm management for safety, but management and boundaries are not the same thing as punishing the dog for communicating discomfort or distress.

Are you a dog trainer?

Training can absolutely be part of behaviour work, but my role is broader than teaching obedience or cues. Behaviour problems often involve fear, stress, frustration, conflict, unmet needs, environment, learning history or relationship dynamics.

I am interested first in understanding what is driving the behaviour. Skills are useful when they support that bigger picture.

Village & free-living dogs

Dogs shaped by a different way of life.

A dog’s background matters. Moving from a more autonomous life into a human household can ask for a huge behavioural and emotional adjustment.

What is a village dog or free-living dog?

On this site, I use these terms for dogs whose lives or ancestry are rooted in free-living or semi-free-living dog populations: dogs who may move through villages, streets, beaches or communities with considerably more autonomy than a typical companion dog.

Some are unowned, some are loosely cared for by communities, and some have close relationships with particular people while still living largely outside the pet-dog model.

Are village dogs a breed?

Not in the simple sense of one recognised breed with one predictable temperament. “Village dog” is often more useful as a description of population, ancestry and way of life.

There is enormous individual variation, so I do not assume that every village dog will behave in the same way.

How can village dogs differ from typical pet dogs?

Many have developed in environments where making their own decisions, avoiding risk, choosing social contact, roaming, scavenging and maintaining distance from unfamiliar people were useful skills.

When we bring a dog with that background into a home, we may suddenly expect comfort with confinement, leads, handling, visitors, close physical contact, separation and a much higher degree of human control. Some dogs adapt easily; others need far more time and support.

Why can adopting a street or village dog be difficult?

Adoption changes far more than where the dog sleeps. The dog may be moving between completely different social, sensory and environmental worlds.

Difficulties can emerge around fear, handling, visitors, being left alone, confinement, reactivity, resource guarding, chasing, roaming or simply coping with expectations they have never encountered before.

Does every free-living dog want to become a pet?

No. Being unowned is not automatically the same as being neglected or wanting a domestic home. Some free-living dogs are deeply embedded in a place, routine and social community that already works for them.

Good intentions matter, but so does context. Before intervening, it is worth understanding the individual dog and the life they already have.

My dog lived in a shelter before adoption. Does their village-dog background still matter?

Yes. Shelter experience adds another layer, but it does not erase what came before it. Some dogs may have spent very little time living freely; others may have years of experience outside a home before entering a shelter.

I look at the whole known history rather than assuming one label explains the dog.

Behaviour questions

What I can help you make sense of.

The behaviour we see is only one part of the picture. The first step is working out what may be maintaining it and what the dog needs from us.

What kinds of behaviour cases do you work with?

I commonly work with fearfulness, anxiety, reactivity, aggression-related behaviour, resource guarding, attachment or separation difficulties, overarousal, roaming, chasing, handling concerns and problems that emerge after rescue or adoption.

If I do not think remote support is appropriate for your situation, I will say so during the discovery process.

Can you help with aggression?

I do work with aggression-related cases where online support is appropriate. Aggression is not a diagnosis or a personality type; it is behaviour that needs context.

Safety and management come first. From there, we look at triggers, function, emotional state, predictability, environment and what realistic progress could look like for that dog.

Will you make my dog stop doing a behaviour?

Sometimes the behaviour does reduce substantially. But I do not begin with “How do we stop this?” I begin with “Why does this make sense for this dog?”

From there we can decide whether the behaviour needs to be changed, managed, accommodated, redirected or whether something in the dog’s environment needs to change first.

What does progress usually look like?

Progress might mean fewer or less intense reactions, better recovery, increased ability to cope, more predictable behaviour, safer management, more communication, greater choice, improved trust or simply a household that understands what to do.

Behaviour change is rarely perfectly linear, so I look for meaningful changes in everyday life rather than perfection.

Working with me

What support looks like in practice.

Online work gives us time to look closely at history, patterns, videos and what is actually happening in the dog’s everyday environment.

How does online behaviour support work?

We meet by video call and go through your dog’s history, current behaviour, environment, routines, triggers and your goals. I then create recommendations tailored to the case.

Depending on your package, we use follow-up calls, WhatsApp support and video review to see what is changing and adjust the plan.

Do you need to meet my dog in person?

Not necessarily. Much of behaviour work is about gathering good information, identifying patterns and changing what happens around the behaviour. Video can also be extremely useful because I can observe interactions in the dog’s normal environment without adding a stranger to the situation.

Some cases do need hands-on local assessment or veterinary involvement, and I will tell you if that becomes important.

What happens during the first consultation?

We spend time understanding the problem in context rather than jumping immediately to exercises. We talk through history, health, environment, routines, relationships, triggers, what you have already tried and what life currently looks like for both you and your dog.

By the end, the aim is for you to understand the behaviour more clearly and know what the next priorities are.

What is the difference between the Clarity Plan and the Transformation Journey?

The Clarity Plan is for guardians who need to understand what is happening, receive a personalised direction and have one month of support while they begin implementing it.

The Transformation Journey is for more complex, persistent or evolving cases that benefit from three months of observation, follow-up, video analysis, troubleshooting and plan adjustments.

Which package should I choose?

You do not need to decide before contacting me. That is exactly what the free 15-minute discovery call is for. We can briefly discuss what is happening and decide which level of support makes sense.

Can I send videos?

Yes. Video review is included to different degrees depending on the package. Short, natural clips can be very useful for understanding body language, context and patterns that are difficult to describe in words.

What happens after my package ends?

If you are doing well, you may not need anything else. Existing clients who want continued professional input can move onto Ongoing Support for maintenance, troubleshooting and help through new challenges.

Philosophy

The ideas behind the work.

Understanding the dog does not mean having no boundaries. It means making decisions with better information.

What does “welfare before obedience” mean?

It means I care more about how the dog is coping than how compliant they look. A dog can obey while still being frightened, overwhelmed or shut down.

Skills matter, but they should support the dog’s wellbeing and the household’s needs rather than becoming the only measure of success.

What does “relationship over control” mean?

It does not mean dogs get to do anything they want. It means the relationship is not built around continually overriding the dog.

I want guardians to become better at reading communication, setting thoughtful boundaries, creating safety and building cooperation that works for both sides.

Does my dog need more boundaries?

Possibly — but “more boundaries” is not a diagnosis. Some dogs benefit from clearer routines, management and predictability. Others are already living under so much restriction and pressure that adding more control makes things harder.

We look at what the individual dog and household actually need.

What if my dog never becomes the dog I imagined?

This can be one of the hardest parts of living with a fearful, independent or complex dog. Sometimes progress includes grieving the dog or lifestyle we expected and learning to see the dog in front of us more clearly.

Acceptance is not the same as giving up. It can be the point where realistic, compassionate behaviour work finally becomes possible.

What does success look like to you?

Success is a dog and human who understand each other better and a life that works more safely and comfortably for both. That might include less distress, more trust, better recovery, clearer communication, greater confidence, more appropriate freedom or simply knowing how to navigate difficult moments without constantly feeling lost.
